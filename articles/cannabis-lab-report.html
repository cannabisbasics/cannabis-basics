How to Read a Cannabis Certificate of Analysis

Match the batch before comparing numbers, and understand what a lab report can answer.

Cannabis Basics · Published October 12, 2026 · 5–7 min + learning checks

A report belongs to a sample

A certificate of analysis, often shortened to COA, records laboratory findings for a particular sample or batch. Maryland's May 2024 final-product-testing bulletin says retail finished products must have an accompanying COA identifying the sample type or item name. A report that looks impressive is not useful if it belongs to a different product or batch.

Start with the product identity, batch or lot identifier, laboratory name and relevant dates. Compare those details with the package. If a retailer shows a generic report for the brand, ask for the report that matches the product in your hand. Do not let a QR code substitute for the matching step: a link can lead to an old, incomplete or unrelated page.

Read units before ranking potency

Look at whether a cannabinoid result is a percentage, milligrams per gram, milligrams per serving or another unit. A number without its unit is easy to misread. Ask how a reported value relates to the actual package and route. Do not compare a flower percentage directly with the milligrams in an edible as though they measured the same experience.

A teaching example: two reports show the number 10. One means 10 percent in flower; the other means 10 milligrams in one edible serving. The shared number does not make them equivalent. Use the label guide and Product Decoder to organize information, then ask the retailer about anything unclear. Calculations about content do not establish a suitable amount for you.

Inspect the testing scope

Maryland laboratory responsibilities include reporting concentrations, contaminants and whether a batch falls within applicable action limits. Check which panels are shown and whether the report includes an overall result. A missing line should prompt a question rather than an assumption that the substance was absent. Report formats and requirements differ, so avoid assuming every state's document has the same headings.

A passing result should be read in the context of the tests and standards used. It is not FDA approval of a treatment and does not guarantee that every possible contaminant was measured. It also does not predict whether a product will cause anxiety, interact with medicine or work for a health goal. Those are different questions requiring different evidence.

Use the report as a consumer skill

Imagine a recall notice appears for a product you bought. A saved label photo, receipt and matching COA make the comparison easier. You can check exact identifiers instead of guessing from a brand name. Keep copies with your journal if that helps you organize products, but avoid collecting personal information you do not need.

When something does not match, pause the purchase conversation and ask the licensed retailer to clarify. Check the relevant regulator's current notices for recalls or testing concerns.
