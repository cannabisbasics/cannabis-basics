The 2026 Cannabis Use Disorder Study: Read the Trend, Not Just the Headline

A new national survey analysis asks how cannabis use disorder changed from 2021 to 2024. Here is what its numbers can—and cannot—tell you.

Cannabis Basics · Source links checked October 9, 2026 · Published October 10, 2026

What the source says

Researchers analyzed survey responses from 186,823 adults in repeated national samples. Estimated cannabis use disorder prevalence was 5.9% in 2021, 7.0% in 2022, 6.9% in 2023 and 7.4% in 2024. These are weighted population estimates, not diagnoses of every person who uses cannabis.

Read a trend without assigning yourself a diagnosis

Imagine a fictional article compares survey findings from two years. A reader sees an upward trend and immediately decides that everybody who uses cannabis has the same problem. The missing step is to examine who was surveyed, what was measured and how the study defined its categories. A population trend can raise useful questions without telling you the status of a particular individual.

Make a small study card with four fields: population, years of data, measure and limitations. Preserve any distinction between all surveyed adults and respondents who used cannabis. Those are different comparison groups. The denominator guide is a useful companion when a percentage seems clear until you ask what the percentage is actually of.

Use reflection to prepare a conversation

Write about your priorities in ordinary language: what you hoped cannabis would help with, what you are noticing now and what you would like to change. Record observations instead of assigning yourself a label from one article. If you want help understanding a pattern, those specifics can make a professional conversation more productive than a general statement that your use is good or bad.

Your decision prompt: are you trying to understand the study or make a decision about your own use? Both are worthwhile, but they require different information. The journal method supports a clearer personal record; the tolerance and withdrawal guide helps frame changes over time. The clinician guide can organize the next discussion. This reflection exercise is not a diagnostic test or a substitute for an assessment.
