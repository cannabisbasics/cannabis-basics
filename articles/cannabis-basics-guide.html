Cannabis Basics: THC, CBD, Terpenes & What a Label Really Tells You

A clear starting point for understanding the plant, the product, and the numbers without treating marketing language like science.

Cannabis Basics · Original guide reviewed September 2026 · Learning features updated October 10, 2026
Key takeawayA cannabis label gives you clues about what is in the product. It does not predict exactly how you will feel. The most useful approach is to read the label, understand the product type, and then compare that information with your own experience.

What exactly are you buying when you buy cannabis?

“Cannabis” sounds like one thing, but the modern market contains many very different products. Dried flower, pre-rolls, vapes, concentrates, edibles, beverages, tinctures, capsules, and topicals can all contain compounds from the same plant while behaving very differently in use.

The cannabis plant contains more than 100 cannabinoids. THC is the compound most associated with intoxication, while CBD is not intoxicating in the same way. Products may also list minor cannabinoids and terpenes. Those details are useful, but they are not a complete forecast of the experience.

CDC notes that the way cannabis affects a person depends on factors such as THC concentration, frequency of use, mode of use, previous experience, biology, and whether cannabis is combined with alcohol or other substances. That is the first principle of Cannabis Basics: the product matters, and the person matters too.

THC: important, but not the whole story

THC stands for tetrahydrocannabinol. It is the cannabinoid most closely associated with the “high” or intoxicating effects of cannabis. On flower, THC is commonly shown as a percentage. On edibles, beverages, tinctures, and some other products, THC is often shown in milligrams per serving and per package.

People understandably focus on THC because it is easy to compare one number with another. But a number can become misleading when it is treated like a universal strength meter. A person with low tolerance can have a very strong response to a product that another experienced user considers mild. A 20% flower product and a 20-milligram edible are not directly comparable because the routes, units, onset, and metabolism are different.

Higher THC also is not automatically “better.” CDC has noted that high-concentration THC products can have greater intoxicating effects and can increase the risk of overconsumption. For a consumer, the better question is: what is the product, how much was used, how quickly did effects appear, and what happened next?

CBD: non-intoxicating does not mean simple

CBD—cannabidiol—does not produce the same intoxicating high associated with THC. It appears in cannabis and hemp products and is heavily marketed for wellness. The important distinction is between what is marketed and what has been demonstrated.

FDA has approved a prescription CBD medicine for specific seizure disorders. That does not mean retail CBD oils, gummies, beverages, creams, and supplements have all been proven safe or effective for the conditions they advertise. Product quality and labeling can vary, and CBD can interact with medications.

If a cannabis product contains both THC and CBD, record both. That information can become useful when you compare several sessions over time.

Terpenes: why cannabis smells the way it does

Terpenes are aromatic compounds found in many plants. Limonene is associated with citrus aromas; pinene with pine; linalool with floral notes; caryophyllene with peppery or spicy notes; myrcene with earthy or musky aromas. Cannabis products increasingly display terpene information because consumers want more detail than a strain name alone.

Terpenes can be useful descriptors, but effect claims should be treated carefully. A package or dispensary menu may describe one terpene as “energizing” and another as “relaxing.” Those descriptions are not guarantees. The science around how multiple cannabis compounds interact is still developing.

Use terpenes as a way to compare aroma profiles and record patterns. If you repeatedly enjoy products with a particular combination, that observation is meaningful for your personal journal even if it does not prove a universal effect.

What about indica, sativa, and hybrid?

These labels remain familiar shopping categories, but they are not dependable enough to predict a precise experience. A person can buy two products both labeled “indica” and have noticeably different responses because the cannabinoid content, terpene profile, potency, harvest, processing, route, and personal context differ.

That does not make the labels useless. They are part of the language of the market. Just treat them as a starting description rather than a promise.

The five-part label reading habit

Before using a product, capture five things:
  • Product name and type
  • THC and CBD as printed
  • Dominant terpenes if listed
  • Serving or package information
  • Your own reason for choosing it and what you want to observe

This habit turns a label from a piece of packaging into useful information. The Cannabis Decoder on this site helps organize those clues, and the journal captures the part the package cannot: your actual experience.

How cannabinoids fit into the bigger picture

THC and CBD get most of the attention, but they are part of a larger family of compounds called cannabinoids. Products may also list CBG, CBN, CBC, THCV, or other compounds. Seeing a new cannabinoid on a package does not automatically mean the product has a proven special benefit. In many cases, research is still developing, product concentrations are small, and marketing language moves faster than high-quality evidence.

A useful consumer rule is to separate three levels of information. First is measured content: what the laboratory or package says is present. Second is research context: what scientists currently know or do not know about that compound. Third is your observation: what you personally noticed after using the product. Mixing those levels together is how a label becomes a promise it was never designed to make.

For example, if a product lists CBN and describes itself as an evening formula, record the CBN and the marketing claim separately. Then record whether you actually became sleepy, relaxed, restless, or felt no clear difference. After several sessions, the journal can tell you whether the claim has been useful for you without pretending that your experience proves the effect for everyone.

What the endocannabinoid system has to do with any of this

Cannabinoids interact with biological systems in the body, including the endocannabinoid system. That system is involved in a wide range of functions, which is part of the reason cannabis can affect mood, memory, appetite, coordination, perception, and other processes. It is also one reason the effects are difficult to reduce to a single “strain effect.”

The important consumer takeaway is not to memorize receptor names. It is to understand that cannabis is acting on a complex biological system, not flipping one simple on/off switch. A product can affect several functions at once, and the balance between desired and unwanted effects can shift with the amount used, route, frequency, and the individual.

That complexity is also why a website should be cautious about turning chemistry into medical claims. A product containing a certain cannabinoid may be interesting, but the presence of that compound alone does not tell you that the product will treat a health condition.

Why the package can look scientific and still leave questions

Modern cannabis packaging often includes percentages, milligrams, terpene lists, batch numbers, testing dates, QR codes, and laboratory names. That can create a strong sense of precision. Some of that precision is valuable: a tested cannabinoid result is more informative than a colorful strain name. But the numbers still have limits.

Testing rules differ by jurisdiction. Products can also vary from batch to batch. The number printed on one package describes that product or batch; it does not establish a universal standard for everything with the same name. A “Gelato” from one producer may not be chemically identical to another producer’s “Gelato.”

Use the data for what it is good at: identifying the product, comparing batches, understanding approximate potency, and documenting what you used. Do not ask it to do what it cannot do: predict your exact mood, guarantee a therapeutic result, or tell you whether a product is appropriate with your medications or health conditions.

A simple first-session workflow

If you are learning a new product, the best time to record information is before memory gets fuzzy. Take a minute to capture the label. Note the product type and the main cannabinoid numbers. If terpenes are listed, record the top one or two. Note the serving language and any warnings. Then write one sentence about what you expect or hope to notice.

After the session, return to the journal and write what actually happened. Do not try to make the experience match the package description. If the menu called it “uplifting” and you felt tired, write tired. If a product was marketed as “calming” and you felt mentally busy, record that. The usefulness of the journal depends on honest observation rather than agreement with the marketing.

Over time, this gives you a personal reference library. You may learn that certain categories work more predictably for you, that very high THC adds no benefit, that a particular route is easier to manage, or that the same product behaves differently depending on sleep, food, and setting.

What a beginner should ignore at first

You do not need to master every cannabinoid abbreviation, memorize every terpene, understand cultivation terminology, or know the genetic family tree of a strain before you can make better observations. Start with product type, THC/CBD, route, serving information, and your own response.

Once those basics feel natural, add more detail. This prevents cannabis education from becoming an overwhelming collection of vocabulary words. The goal is not to sound like an expert at a dispensary. The goal is to understand enough to make the label useful and to create a record you can return to.
